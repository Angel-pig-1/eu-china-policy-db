COMMISSION STAFF WORKING DOCUMENT

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Proposal for a Regulation of the European Parliament and of the Council on the safety, resilience and sustainability of space activities in the Union

{SWD(2025) 335 final}

Executive Summary

Impact assessment report (Part 1/2)

Lead DG: DG DEFIS

1. Introduction: Political and legal context

Space is a strategic domain for Europe's security, competitiveness, and technological sovereignty. The global space economy is expected to grow significantly, driving innovation and economic growth. At the same time, the increasing congestion of orbital environment, the growing number of actors, space debris, risks of collision and interferences raise significant challenges. Space technologies and services are also indispensable enablers for the EU's security and defence.

The proposal for a Regulation of the European Parliament and of the Council on the safety, resilience and sustainability of space activities in the Union (referred to as the EU Space Act) intends to create a harmonised framework for space activities in the EU, in particular for the authorisation and supervision of space activities, space debris mitigation, cybersecurity and environmental sustainability.

Political context: European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen instructed Commissioner Andrius Kubilius in her mission letter to prepare an EU Space Act in order to foster a globally competitive and resilient space sector. Commission President von der Leyen announced the EU Space Act in her 2025 State of the Union Address. Competitiveness Compass Communication (2025) also announced the EU Space Act. In her Political Guidelines, President von der Leyen outlined her vision for Europe's space policy, including the development of an EU space law to reduce market fragmentation, address the increasing risk of collisions and the lack of proper disposal of space objects after the end of their operational life, and mitigate the environmental impact of the sector while strengthening its competitiveness and resilience.

The EU Space Act follows on recent major policy and strategy initiatives relating to the EU space policy (EU Space Strategy for Security and Defence, EU Space Traffic Management approach, White Paper for Space-based Research, Innovation and Economy). The EU Space Act seeks to establish a harmonised, predictable and innovation-friendly framework, while ensuring the highest safety, resilience and sustainability standards. It will boost innovation, attract investment and enhance the EU's strategic autonomy in space. It will ensure the security and sustainability of space activities, protecting critical infrastructure, mitigating risks of collisions and space debris, and safeguarding the environment.

Legal context: Space activities are currently regulated at national level in 13 Member States (Austria, Belgium, Czechia, Denmark, France, Germany, Greece, Finland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Poland, Portugal, Romania, Sweden) as well as at the level of the United Kingdom and Norway. While national laws broadly share similar objectives, they differ significantly in scope and approach. There is currently no overarching harmonised EU-wide framework for the authorisation and supervision of space activities. There is a need to establish a common approach for common safety, resilience and sustainability requirements in the internal market.

An Impact Assessment accompanied the 2016 Space Strategy for Europe and underpinned the Space Regulation (2018), but it did not address these challenges as it did not cover non-EU governmental systems, nor the sustainable, safety and resilience dimensions in a comprehensive manner.

International law requires States to authorise and supervise national space activities, to avoid harmful interferences, and ensure the safety of operations. States remain internationally liable for damage caused by their space objects. The Outer Space Treaty (1967), the Liability Convention (1972), and the Registration Convention (1975) are the key UN treaties. The EU and its Member States are Parties to these treaties. In addition, several EU Member States are party to the Moon Agreement (1979).

The EU's accession to the European Convention for the Protection of Human Rights and Fundamental Freedoms (ECHR) would make the EU's Charter of Fundamental Rights the equivalent of the EU "bill of rights". The ECHR has an impact on the interpretation of the European Convention on Human Rights and the Charter of Fundamental Rights by the European Court of Human Rights and the Court of Justice of the European Union.

The proposal builds on existing legislation: the European Union Space Programme Regulation (EUSPR, Regulation (EU) 2021/696), GOVSATCOM Regulation ( Regulation 2023/588), EU SST Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2021/696), the NIS2 Directive (Directive (EU) 2022/2555), and others. Relevant bilateral/regional: ESA-EU Framework Agreement.

2. Problem definition

Problem 1: Incoherent authorisation and supervision of space activities in the EU internal market

Thirteen Member States have national space laws, while fourteen have none. National regimes differ significantly in scope, approach and implementation, creating additional costs and delays for operators, uncertainty about applicable rules, forum shopping, and diverging levels of safety and resilience across the EU.

Existing national space laws broadly share similar objectives but differ significantly in scope and approach:

  • Some apply only to launches from their territory; others apply to nationals operating abroad, or to activities conducted under their jurisdiction or control.
  • Some require an authorisation for all space activities; others only for launch.
  • They differ in the treatment of ownership transfer, insurance requirements, and liability caps.

Several space companies have stated that the lack of a harmonised framework creates significant additional costs and delays for their activities. The fragmented landscape creates uncertainty about which rules apply to whom and when. This leads to legal uncertainty, duplicative procedures, and increased operational costs.

The absence of a common EU approach to the authorisation and supervision of space activities leads to diverging safety and resilience standards across the EU.

Fragmentation costs: For large constellation operators, the costs of compliance with multiple national regimes is significant. Space debris mitigation requirements differ across Member States. There are no harmonised requirements for cybersecurity, the sustainability of space activities and of the outer space environment.

A common misconception outside of the space industry is that space debris is not a big problem because of the sheer size of outer space. However, the sheer number of objects, especially in the Low Earth Orbit (LEO), poses significant collision risks.

Problem 2: Lack of common EU requirements for the safety, resilience and sustainability of space activities

The 13 Member States with national space laws broadly share similar objectives but differ significantly in scope and approach. There are no harmonised requirements for cybersecurity, the sustainability of space activities and of the outer space environment. The 2022 Council Conclusions on "Orientations on the EU Space Programme for the next decade" highlighted the need for a clear framework for space traffic management, space debris mitigation and in-space servicing.

Space debris: The proliferation of space debris poses significant risks to operational satellites and space infrastructure. The Kessler Syndrome - a cascading chain of collisions rendering certain orbits unusable - is a growing concern. Studies project that LEO will become increasingly congested, and without intervention, the number of catastrophic collisions is expected to increase. Over 10,000 satellites are currently in orbit. According to ESA's Space Environment Report 2025, the number of objects in orbit has grown dramatically.

Cybersecurity threats: Space systems face increasing cyber threats. Cyber-attacks on space systems can have cascading effects on critical infrastructure and services on Earth. The Viasat KA-SAT cyberattack (2022) demonstrated the vulnerability of space systems to cyber threats. The NIS2 Directive establishes horizontal cybersecurity requirements, but there are no space-specific cybersecurity requirements at EU level.

Environmental sustainability: Space activities have environmental impacts throughout their lifecycle, including atmospheric emissions from launches, debris, and the effects of satellite re-entries on the atmosphere. There are no harmonised requirements for measuring and mitigating the environmental footprint of space activities.

Problem 3: Insufficient EU-level framework to protect the EU's security and strategic interests

Space technologies and services are critical for the EU's security and defence. The EU's growing dependence on space-based services creates vulnerabilities that must be addressed. There are no harmonised mechanisms to ensure that space activities do not compromise the EU's security and strategic interests.

The lack of a common approach to third country operators and investors creates risks for the EU's strategic autonomy. The current fragmented approach weakens the EU's position in international fora and vis-à-vis third countries.

3. Why should the EU act?

The EU has exclusive competence for the establishment and functioning of the internal market. The proposal is based on Article 114 TFEU (harmonisation of national rules for the establishment and functioning of the internal market). Space activities fall within the scope of the internal market as economic activities.

The EU's action is justified on the following grounds:

Subsidiarity: Due to the transnational nature of space activities and the fact that several Member States have not yet developed a national space law, action at EU level is necessary to achieve the objectives of the internal market and to ensure a level playing field.

Proportionality: The proposal complies with the proportionality principle because it does not go beyond what is necessary in order to achieve the objectives. It establishes a harmonised framework while respecting national competences and taking into account the specificities of the space sector.

EU added value: The EU is the only level at which harmonisation can be achieved effectively. National action alone cannot address the transboundary challenges of space activities. The EU's action will create a genuine internal market for space activities, enhance the EU's strategic autonomy, and strengthen the EU's position in international fora.

4. Policy objectives

The general objective of the proposal is to ensure the safe, resilient and sustainable development of space activities in the EU, while fostering the competitiveness of the EU space sector and the internal market for space activities.

The specific objectives are:

  • To establish a harmonised framework for the authorisation and supervision of space activities in the EU.
  • To ensure common safety, resilience and sustainability requirements for space activities.
  • To protect the EU's security and strategic interests in the field of space.
  • To enhance the global competitiveness of the EU space sector.

5. Policy options

Baseline: The "no policy change" option would mean continuation of the status quo, with increasing fragmentation, rising costs for operators, growing risks from space debris and cybersecurity threats, and continued divergence in safety standards. This is not a viable option given the expected growth in space activities.

Option 1: Soft law approach (recommendations/guidelines) – non-binding measures to encourage convergence of national approaches. This would not address the fragmentation problem effectively and would not provide legal certainty.

Option 2: Harmonisation through a Regulation (targeted harmonisation) – establishment of a common framework for authorisation, safety, resilience and sustainability requirements, while respecting national specificities and existing national regimes.

Option 3: Full harmonisation through a Regulation with an EU-level licensing system (centralised EU agency issuing authorisations) – a single EU-level authorisation system replacing national authorisations.

Option 4: Harmonisation through a Directive – setting common objectives to be transposed into national law, allowing for more flexibility but resulting in slower and potentially divergent implementation.

The preferred option is Option 2 (harmonisation through a Regulation), which provides the best balance between harmonisation and flexibility.

7. Preferred option and its impacts

The preferred option (Option 2) is a Regulation establishing:

  • A harmonised framework for the authorisation and supervision of space activities.
  • Common safety requirements (collision avoidance, debris mitigation, tracking).
  • Cybersecurity requirements for space systems.
  • Environmental sustainability requirements (environmental footprint measurement and mitigation).
  • A Union Registry of Space Objects (URSO).
  • Provisions on third country operators.

Expected impacts:

Economic impacts: Reduction of compliance costs for operators, especially SMEs. Creation of a level playing field and increased legal certainty. Stimulation of investment and innovation in the EU space sector. The proposal is expected to have a positive impact on the competitiveness of the EU space sector.

Social impacts: Enhanced safety of space activities, protection of critical infrastructure, and benefits for citizens relying on space-based services (navigation, communication, Earth observation).

Environmental impacts: Mitigation of space debris generation, reduction of the environmental footprint of space activities, and protection of the space environment for future generations.

Security impacts: Enhanced resilience of space systems against cyber and physical threats, protection of the EU's strategic interests, and strengthened EU strategic autonomy.

Impact on SMEs: The proposal includes specific provisions to reduce the administrative burden for SMEs, including simplified procedures for certain categories of space activities.

8. Monitoring and evaluation

The proposal includes provisions for monitoring and evaluation, with regular reporting by the Commission to the European Parliament and the Council. The monitoring framework will include indicators on the number of authorisations, compliance rates, space debris mitigation, and the competitiveness of the EU space sector.

9. Conclusion

The impact assessment concludes that the proposed Regulation is the most effective and proportionate way to address the identified problems. It will create a harmonised framework for space activities in the EU, enhance the safety, resilience and sustainability of space activities, and strengthen the EU's global competitiveness in the space sector.
